What Markus Persson lost by selling Minecraft

June 10, 2025 · 2 min read

Originally posted on LinkedIn, June 10, 2025.

My 8-year-old son plays Minecraft. It took me a while to understand why, because the game has no defined goal.

You build blocks or break them apart on a screen. As adults, it takes us time to understand the pleasure of creating without a purpose or a predefined objective. It is digital Lego. You do not need to defeat anyone or overcome a complex challenge.

The story of how the world's most famous game came to be is an interesting one. Markus Persson was an ordinary programmer who loved computer games. In 2009, he built Minecraft almost entirely by himself, with no advertising, no publishers, and no focus groups. Just open-ended imagination.

Within a few years, it had become a worldwide phenomenon, selling about 15,000 copies a day. Mojang was a small studio with 40 employees and huge profits.

But Markus did not want to run a business, and it frustrated him. He did not want meetings, HR problems, or lawsuits. He wanted to build games, not run an empire.

In 2014, he tweeted half-jokingly: "Is anyone interested in buying my share of Mojang?"

Microsoft answered seriously, and the negotiations moved quickly. The deal closed at $2.5 billion, with Markus taking home about $1.6 billion after taxes.

After the sale, he bought a $70 million mansion in Los Angeles, one of the city's most expensive home purchases at the time. It had candy rooms, car turntables, and panoramic views of LA.

It sounds like a fairy tale. Then came the tweets:

"Hanging out in Ibiza with a bunch of friends and partying with famous people, able to do whatever I want, and I've never felt more isolated."

"The problem with getting everything is you run out of reasons to keep trying."

Markus had lost what drove him to build the game in the first place: community, purpose, and creation. The money solved every problem, but it also removed his reason to keep trying.

He tried investing in startups, developing new games, and going to parties, but nothing brought back the same spark. When he sold Minecraft, he lost something he had not realized he needed most. Something all of us need.

That is the irony. The thing we chase frustrates those who have not achieved it, and as Markus's story shows, it can also frustrate those who have.

I look at my son building purposeless worlds out of blocks and realize they may not be so purposeless after all. Maybe real meaning begins when there is no obvious goal.
